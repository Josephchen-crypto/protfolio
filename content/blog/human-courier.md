## Human Courier

This was the first urgent job I took on after leaving software for the textile business.

The client needed the goods fast. No courier service could guarantee the timing, so we had no choice — human delivery. Me, personally, carrying the shipment.

I overslept that morning. Something kept me up until 4 AM the night before, so I didn't wake up until 9. After washing up, I swung by my parents' place, grabbed a quick lunch near their house, and drove straight to the factory.

The factory finished the laser cutting and trimming on the lace before 11. We counted, packed, loaded the bags into the car, and I set off for the high-speed rail station.

When I pulled the two bags out of the car at the station, I realized — I couldn't carry them alone. I had no idea how much they weighed in total, but at my age, in this body, there was no way. Fifteen years of sitting at a desk. My abs were about as real as a programmer's abs — oh wait, I am a programmer. Never mind. I don't have abs.

I rebooked my ticket a few times, and eventually found a paid porter through the station service desk. He pulled a cart, I followed. We sailed through the entrance, the ticket gates, the green channel, and onto the platform. He even helped me load the bags onto the train — just in time for the last service of the day. Without him, no way I would have made it on board.

I grabbed a quick dinner on the train, around 40 yuan. Train food is genuinely overpriced, but the taste was fine — everything's acceptable if you ignore the price.

I arrived late at night. The client couldn't wait and had already sent someone to the exit. My sister drove up with one of her employees. We took the bags out and handed them off that same night. Then we drove another hour and a half from Shaoxing to Yiwu. My brother had already booked a hotel. Straight to check-in.

The hotel was inside Yiwu's international trade center. The dining room was full of foreigners — mostly Middle Eastern and Indian faces, presumably buyers.

I woke up early the next day. After breakfast at the hotel, I walked around the neighborhood a bit, then headed to my brother's shop. My sister patiently walked me through the very basics of the textile industry. My brain was absorbing everything at full speed. I hadn't slept well the night before — the hotel bed was unfamiliar, my head felt a bit dull — but the training from years of work still kicked in. My ability to understand and learn just barely kept up.

The original plan was two days in Yiwu. I was supposed to take the train home the second day, but my brother asked me to stay an extra day. Said we should talk, just the two of us.

We talked for a long time after dinner. He was mostly worried about where I am right now. He kept saying: settle down, plant your roots in one industry. Plant a tree, not rice. Rice — you harvest it once and then you have to replow and reseed. Over and over.

The logic is obvious. But how many people actually get the chance to plant a tree? How many people, because of how life really works, have to give up the long game and just get through the immediate mess?

I didn't answer.

The third morning, I picked a relaxed train schedule. With time to spare, I walked around the rest of the trade center. Saw a lot of interesting stuff — tried AI glasses, robots, drones. AI glasses do have some convenience for people in foreign trade, but they're nowhere near "can't do business without them" territory.

The train pulled out. First human courier run to Yiwu — done.
